Who Uses LinkedIn? Predicting LinkedIn Use from Demographics¶

Programming II Final Project, Part 1
Authors: Muhammad Faateh Awan, Jack Hamilton, Siho Lee, Pedro Sanchez, Tina Chen

Business context¶

Our marketing team is deciding which platforms to use for campaigns. The CEO asked us to study social media habits in the US public and build a model that predicts whether a person uses LinkedIn from basic demographics.

This notebook does three things:

  1. Cleans the Pew survey data and keeps six features: income, education, parent, married, female and age.
  2. Explores how each feature relates to LinkedIn use.
  3. Builds and tests a logistic regression model, then uses it to score example people.

The same model powers the Streamlit app in Part 2.

Q2. The clean_sm function¶

Define a function called clean_sm that takes one input, x, and uses np.where to check whether x is equal to 1. If it is, make the value of x = 1, otherwise make it 0. Return x. Create a toy dataframe with three rows and two columns and test your function.

def clean_sm(x):
    x = np.where(x == 1, 1, 0)

3.1 Cleaning¶

In [5]:
ss = pd.DataFrame()

# target is LinkedIn user or not
ss["sm_li"] = clean_sm(s["web1h"])

# anything above the top category is missing for income and education
ss["income"] = np.where(s["income"] > 9, np.nan, s["income"])
ss["education"] = np.where(s["educ2"] > 8, np.nan, s["educ2"])

# yes/no features, where 8 and 9 are don't know or refused
ss["parent"] = np.where(s["par"] > 2, np.nan, clean_sm(s["par"]))
ss["married"] = np.where(s["marital"] > 6, np.nan, clean_sm(s["marital"]))

# female is coded 2 in the raw data
ss["female"] = np.where(s["gender"] > 3, np.nan, np.where(s["gender"] == 2, 1, 0))

# age 98 means don't know
ss["age"] = np.where(s["age"] >= 98, np.nan, s["age"])

# counts missing values per column, then drops them

Two cleaning choices we made

  • Don't know and refused answers. The assignment only gives missing rules for income, education and age. We also dropped people who answered "Don't know" or "Refused" for parent, marital status or gender, because refusing to answer is not the same as saying no. This cost us only 10 rows (1,250 instead of 1,260).
  • Age 98. In the codebook, 98 means "Don't know" and 97 is the highest real age. We therefore treated 98 and above as missing.

Most of the dropped rows come from income, which 229 people did not report. We are left with 1,250 people.

3.6 What the exploration tells us¶

  • Education is the strongest signal. Only 12% of high school graduates (level 3) use LinkedIn, compared with 47% of people with a bachelor's degree (level 6) and 47% of people with a postgraduate degree (level 8). Almost nobody without a high school diploma uses it (0% to 3%). The 62% peak at level 7 is based on only 34 people, so it should be read with care.
  • Income works the same way. LinkedIn use is 11% in the lowest income group and 57% in the highest. There is a clear jump at level 7 ($75k and above), where use roughly doubles from about 20% to 44%.
  • Age matters mostly after 65. Use is fairly flat for ages 18 to 64 (37% to 41%) and then falls to 18% for people 65 and older. The median user is 47 years old and the median non-user is 56.
  • The three binary features are weaker. Parents (42% vs 31%) and married people (38% vs 30%) use LinkedIn a little more, and women a little less than men (29% vs 36%).
  • Correlations agree. Income (0.33) and education (0.31) have the strongest link with LinkedIn use, and age is negative (-0.16). Income and education are also related to each other, so the model will share the effect between them.

Takeaway: LinkedIn users are more educated, higher earning and of working age. These features carry real information, so a model built on them should be useful.

The model makes more false positives (45) than false negatives (29). This is a direct result of the balanced class weights: the model is tuned to find users, so it would rather flag too many people than miss real users. For a marketing team that wants reach, this is a reasonable trade.

Accuracy on its own can mislead here, because predicting "not a user" for everyone would already score 66.4%. Precision, recall and F1 in Q9 give a fuller picture.

Q9. Precision, recall and F1 score¶

Use the results in the confusion matrix to calculate each of these metrics by hand. Discuss each metric and give an example of when it might be the preferred metric. Then create a classification_report and check that the metrics match.

9.1 Calculating by hand¶

In [18]:
tn, fp, fn, tp = cm.ravel()

precision = tp / (tp + fp)
recall = tp / (tp + fn)
f1 = 2 * (precision * recall) / (precision + recall)

print(f"TN = {tn}, FP = {fp}, FN = {fn}, TP = {tp}\n")
print(f"Precision = TP / (TP + FP) = {tp} / ({tp} + {fp}) = {precision:.3f}")
print(f"Recall    = TP / (TP + FN) = {tp} / ({tp} + {fn}) = {recall:.3f}")
print(f"F1 score  = 2 x (P x R) / (P + R) = 2 x ({precision:.3f} x {recall:.3f}) / ({precision:.3f} + {recall:.3f}) = {f1:.3f}")

TN = 121, FP = 45, FN = 29, TP = 55

Precision = TP / (TP + FP) = 55 / (55 + 45) = 0.550
Recall    = TP / (TP + FN) = 55 / (55 + 29) = 0.655
F1 score  = 2 x (P x R) / (P + R) = 2 x (0.550 x 0.655) / (0.550 + 0.655) = 0.598

9.2 What each metric means¶

Precision = 0.550. Of the 100 people the model called LinkedIn users, 55 really are.
When to prefer it: when a false positive is expensive. Example: a paid LinkedIn ad campaign with a small budget, where every person we wrongly target is wasted money. A spam filter is another case, because sending a real email to the spam folder is costly.

Recall = 0.655. Of the 84 real LinkedIn users in the test set, the model found 55.
When to prefer it: when missing a positive is expensive. Example: disease screening, where missing a sick patient is much worse than sending a healthy one for a second test. In marketing, recall matters for a cheap, broad awareness campaign where we want to reach as many real users as possible.

F1 score = 0.598. The harmonic mean of precision and recall. It is only high when both are high.
When to prefer it: when the classes are imbalanced and both kinds of error matter, so one number is needed to compare models. Example: fraud detection, where blocking honest customers and missing real fraud are both costly. It is also the fairest single number for this project, because only one third of people are users.

Two cautions when reading these numbers

  1. The probabilities are relative scores. Because of class_weight="balanced", the model's probabilities run higher than the true rates (the true overall rate is 33%). They are good for ranking people and for the yes/no classification, but 71.9% should not be read as "72 in 100 people like this use LinkedIn".
  2. This is a pattern, not a cause. The model shows that older people in this survey use LinkedIn less. It does not prove that ageing makes someone leave LinkedIn. Many older respondents are retired, and work status is not in the model.

Conclusion and recommendations for the marketing team¶

What we found

  • About one in three US adults in the survey uses LinkedIn.
  • LinkedIn users are more educated, higher earning and of working age. Use is 47% among bachelor's degree holders compared with 12% among high school graduates, and it more than doubles once household income passes $75k.
  • Use drops sharply after 65 (18%).
  • Gender, marital status and being a parent matter much less.

How good is the model?

  • Accuracy 70%, recall 65%, precision 55%, F1 0.60 on people it had never seen.
  • It finds about two out of three real LinkedIn users. About half of the people it flags are real users, compared with one in three if we picked people at random.

Recommendations

  1. Use LinkedIn for campaigns aimed at college-educated professionals with household income above $75k, aged roughly 25 to 64. This is where LinkedIn reach is strongest.
  2. Do not rely on LinkedIn to reach people over 65 or people without a college education. Other platforms should be considered for those segments.
  3. Use the model as a first screen, not a final answer. It is good for ranking segments by likely LinkedIn use. For expensive, tightly targeted campaigns, raise the probability cut-off above 50% to improve precision.

Limits and next steps

  • The model uses only six demographic features. Employment status, industry and job type would likely improve it a lot.
  • The data is a single Pew survey, so results should be refreshed as platform use changes.
  • Part 2 turns this model into an interactive Streamlit app, so the marketing team can enter a customer profile and see the prediction in real time.
